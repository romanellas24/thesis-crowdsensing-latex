\chapter{Protocol design} \label{ch:protocol-design}
In this chapter the protocol design is presented.
The protocol leverages smart contracts and asymmetric encryption to ensure confidentiality and data integrity.
The protocol adopts a multi-layered encryption approach, performed off-chain, and uses the smart contract as a communication channel for the crowdsensing campaign.
The key exchange is made to and from the campaign initiator (referred from now as ''\texttt{B}'').
\texttt{B} uses their private/public key pair for enhancing confidentiality, in this way, only \texttt{B} can access the other actors' public key, namely senders (\texttt{S}) and verifiers (\texttt{V}).
This approach shares similarities with prior work on asymmetric key exchange mediated by smart contracts~\cite{muth2020smartdhx}, although it differs substantially in both application domain and protocol design.

\section{Actors}\label{sec:actors}
The protocol involves several actors that are described in the following subsections.

\subsection{Campaign initiator}\label{subsec:campaign-initiator}
The campaign initiator (\texttt{B}) is the actor interested for collecting data for some reason.
They define the geographic area where the smart contract works and set the smart contract hyperparameters, all configurable parameters are explained in Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-a-parameters}.
In addition, they deposit the reward funds into the smart contract to ensure that funds are available and senders and verifiers can be compensated.
The details of rewarding model are described in the~\ref{subsec:proto-rewarding-model} subsection.

\subsection{Sender}\label{subsec:sender}
Senders (hereafter referred to as \("\)\texttt{S}\("\)) are the users who contribute to the crowdsensing campaign by submitting data retrieved from the environment where they are located.
To contribute, senders, after the joining phase, upload data to IPFS and submit the encrypted CID through the smart contract.

\subsection{Verifier}\label{subsec:verifiers}
Verifiers (hereafter referred to as \("\)\texttt{V}\("\)) are the users who contribute to the crowdsensing campaign by executing data quality checks and submitting the verification result through smart contract functions.
To contribute, verifiers listen for events emitted by the smart contract.
Upon receiving a \texttt{DataVerificationRequestedEvent}, they request verification authorization; if granted, they execute the verification and publish the result.

\subsection{Smart Contract}\label{subsec:smartcontract}
Smart Contract (hereafter referred to as \("\)\texttt{SC}\("\)) is the actor that mediates the interaction between all other actors.
In addition, this actor handle the payment model and the bond mechanism, described in Sections ~\ref{subsec:proto-rewarding-model} and ~\ref{subsec:proto-bond-model}.
The smart contract acts as the sole communication channel between participants, eliminating direct interaction.
It stores encrypted cryptographic material and data references on-chain, and enforces campaign lifecycle rules such as participation thresholds and automatic closure.

\section{Chunking system}\label{sec:chunking-system}
A fundamental challenge in implementing spatial crowdsensing on a smart contract is the lack of native floating-point support in the Solidity language~\cite{wood2014ethereum}.
Since geographic coordinates are inherently expressed as real numbers, a coordinate remapping strategy is required to represent them as integers without loss of precision.
This section describes the spatial partitioning model adopted by the smart contract and the fixed-point arithmetic used to handle geographic coordinates.

\subsection{Coordinate remapping}\label{subsec:coordinate-remapping}
Since Solidity does not support floating-point arithmetic natively as stated before, geographic coordinates are transmitted by clients as a
\texttt{GlobalPosition} struct composed of four fields:
the integer and decimal parts of latitude, and the integer and decimal parts of longitude,where the decimal component assumes a precision of up to seven digits after the decimal point.

To enable arithmetic operations within the EVM, coordinates are converted into a compact signed integer representation known as E7 format, where one unit
corresponds to $10^{-7}$ degrees, providing a spatial resolution of approximately $1.1$\,cm.
The conversion is performed by the \texttt{packCoordinates} function, which produces a \texttt{PackedPosition}
struct containing two \texttt{int32} fields, \texttt{latE7} and \texttt{lonE7}.

For a non-negative integer part, the packed value is computed as:

\[
    \textit{(lon/lat)E7} = \textit{integer} \times 10^7 + \textit{decimal}
\]

For a negative integer part, the decimal component is subtracted to correctly
extend the magnitude:

\[
    \textit{(lon/lat)E7} = \textit{integer} \times 10^7 - \textit{decimal}
\]

This representation preserves the full signed range of geographic coordinates without requiring any coordinate shifting,
and fits within a 32-bit signed integer for the valid ranges of both latitude ($[-90\text{°}, +90\text{°}]$) and longitude ($[-180\text{°}, +180\text{°}]$)~\cite{wgs84}.

\subsection{Spatial partitioning}\label{subsec:spatial-partitioning}
The operational area of the smart contract is modelled as a square region subdivided into a uniform grid of $n \times n$ square chunks, where $n$ denotes the number of chunks per side (\texttt{chunk4Side}).
Each chunk represents a distinct geographic portion of the territory.
The origin of the grid is located at the south-west corner of the area; latitude increases northward and longitude increases eastward from the origin.
At deployment time, the end coordinate is derived by adding the total area extent to both axes of the packed origin:

\[
    \textit{end} = \textit{origin} + \texttt{chunkSize} \times \texttt{chunk4Side}
\]

If no spatial subdivision is required, setting $n = 1$ yields a single chunk
covering the entire operational area, preserving compatibility with deployments
that do not use the chunking mechanism.

\subsection{Chunk indexing}\label{subsec:chunk-indexing}

Given a \texttt{PackedPosition}, its chunk coordinates are computed as:

\begin{equation}
    \texttt{row} = \left\lfloor \frac{\textit{lat}_{E7} -
    \textit{origin.lat}_{E7}}{\texttt{chunkSize}} \right\rfloor, \qquad
    \texttt{col} = \left\lfloor \frac{\textit{lon}_{E7} -
    \textit{origin.lon}_{E7}}{\texttt{chunkSize}} \right\rfloor
    \label{eq:row-col}
\end{equation}

Since latitude increases northward, row~$0$ corresponds to the southernmost
strip; since longitude increases eastward, column~$0$ corresponds to the
westernmost strip.
Each chunk is then assigned a unique sequential index in the range $[0,\ n^2 - 1]$:

\begin{equation}
    \textit{chunk\_index} = \texttt{row} \times n + \texttt{col}
    \label{eq:chunk-index}
\end{equation}
Where $n$ is the number is \texttt{chunk4Side}, described in Table~\ref{tab:params-chunks}.

This linear mapping establishes a direct correspondence between the
two-dimensional grid coordinates and a one-dimensional array, enabling
constant-time lookup of chunk data from its geographic position.
A data submission is rejected if the packed coordinates fall outside the bounds
$[\textit{origin}, \textit{end})$ on either axis.


\section{Protocol workflow}\label{sec:proto-workflow}
The protocol workflow is described in the following subsections.
Each phase is illustrated by a corresponding sequence diagram.
The protocol design follows and extends the work presented in~\cite{bedogni2024crowdsensing}.

For clarity, the following notation is adopted throughout this chapter:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\text{Enc}(m, PK)$: encryption of message $m$ using public key $PK$;
    \item $\text{Dec}(c, SK)$: decryption of ciphertext $c$ using private key $SK$;
    \item $(PK_X, SK_X)$: public and private key pair of actor $X$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Initialization}\label{subsec:proto-init}
Initialization phase consists of the following steps.
The campaign initiator (\texttt{B}) deploys the smart contract, indicating all the parameters, described in Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-a-parameters}.
Before the deployment, \texttt{B} generates its pair of private/public key $(PK_B, SK_B)$ which is used for sender and verifiers enrollment (explained respectively in Section~\ref{subsec:proto-sender-enrollment} and Section~\ref{subsec:proto-verifier-enrollment}) and generates also an asymmetric key pair $(K_{enc}, K_{dec})$.
After the deployment, the owner approves the transfer of a \texttt{totalDst}
quantity of DST (described in~\ref{subsec:data-sent-token}) to the smart contract address,
and then confirms the deposit.

\subsection{Sender enrollment}\label{subsec:proto-sender-enrollment}
Any sender who wants to contribute to the crowdsensing campaign has to generate a private/public key pair. $(PK_S, SK_S)$
Subsequently, \texttt{S} retrieves the public key of \texttt{B} from the smart contract.
Then \texttt{S} computes:
\[
    c^{S}_{1} = \text{Enc}(PK_S, PK_B)
\]
and sends $c^{S}_{1}$ to the smart contract.
Upon receiving the ciphertext, the smart contract emits an event that \texttt{B} monitors.
When \texttt{B} detects the event, it retrieves the sender address and $c^{S}_{1}$from it.

Then \texttt{B} computes:
\[
    PK_S = \text{Dec}(c^{S}_{1}, SK_B)
\]
At this point, \texttt{B} possesses \texttt{S}'s public key and can establish a secure channel. \texttt{B} computes:
\[
    c^{S}_{2} = \text{Enc}(K_{enc}, PK_S)
\]
And sends $c^{S}_{2}$ to the smart contract which triggers an event to notify \texttt{S}.
Finally, \texttt{S} retrieves $c^{S}_{2}$ from the emitted event and computes
\[
    K_{enc} = \text{Dec}(c^{S}_{2}, SK_S)
\]
This key will be used for CID encryption, as shown in Section~\ref{subsec:proto-datasubmit}.
All interactions are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:proto-design-workflow-sender-join}

\subsection{Verifier enrollment}\label{subsec:proto-verifier-enrollment}
Verifiers contribute to crowdsensing campaign by performing data quality checks on the data.
Any verifier who wants to contribute to the crowdsensing campaign has to generate a private/public key pair $(PK_V, SK_V)$.
Subsequently, \texttt{V} retrieves the public key of \texttt{B} from the smart contract.
Then \texttt{V} computes:
\[
    c^{V}_{1} = \text{Enc}(PK_V, PK_B)
\]
then sends $c^{V}_{1}$ to the smart contract.

Upon receiving the ciphertext, the smart contract emits an event that \texttt{B} monitors.
When \texttt{B} detects the event, it retrieves the verifier address and $c^{V}_{1}$ from it.

Then \texttt{B} computes:
\[
    PK_V = \text{Dec}(c^{V}_{1}, SK_B)
\]
At this point, \texttt{B} possesses \texttt{V}'s public key and can establish a secure channel. \texttt{B} computes:
\[
    c^{V}_{2} = \text{Enc}(K_{dec}, PK_V)
\]
and sends $c^{V}_{2}$ to the smart contract which triggers an event to notify \texttt{V}.
Finally, \texttt{V} retrieves $c^{V}_{2}$ from the emitted event and computes
\[
    K_{dec} = \text{Dec}(c^{V}_{2}, SK_V)
\]
This key will be used for CID decryption, as shown in Section~\ref{subsec:proto-verification}.

\subsection{Data submission}\label{subsec:proto-datasubmit}
Data submission is the main process of the protocol.
It enables senders to contribute data references to the campaign.
When \texttt{S} has data for the campaign, it encrypts data with $K_{enc}$ key and uploads the encrypted data to IPFS.
IPFS returns a Content Identifier (CID, see Section~\ref{subsec:ipfs})
\[
    CID_{ciphered} = \text{Enc}(\text{CID}, K_{enc})
\]
Then \texttt{S} sends $CID_{ciphered}$ to the smart contract along with the geographic position of the measurement.
Once the data is registered, a specific event is emitted by the smart contract which is monitored by verifiers.
This concludes the data submission phase and triggers the verification phase (described in Section ~\ref{subsec:proto-verification}).

\subsection{Verification}\label{subsec:proto-verification}
The verification phase is performed by verifiers, whose goal is to determine whether a given data entry meets the quality requirements of the campaign.
To mitigate the risk of a single malicious or faulty verifier, each data entry is independently verified through multiple \textit{verification slots}.
The number of slots is conFigured to be odd, so that a strict majority decision is always reachable and ties are structurally avoided.
Slots are not statically assigned: each verifier must explicitly request authorization, and the smart contract elects it to an available slot only if the eligibility and cooldown constraints are satisfied.

A verifier \texttt{V} can request authorization either upon receiving a \texttt{DataVerificationRequestedEvent} or by polling autonomously.
Please note that the smart contract is not constrained to return the data entry that triggered the event: any pending data entry eligible for verification can be returned.

The authorization request has two possible outcomes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The \texttt{VerifierElected} event is emitted, carrying among its parameters the ciphered CID and the address of the elected verifier;
    \item The authorization is denied, in which case \texttt{V} must wait before attempting again.
\end{itemize}

When \texttt{V} is elected, it can proceed with the verification.
\texttt{V} computes:
\[
    CID = \text{Dec}(CID_{ciphered}, K_{dec})
\]
\texttt{V} then retrieves the encrypted data from IPFS using the $CID$ and performs quality checks on it.
After the checks, \texttt{V} submits the verification result to the smart contract.

As soon as a strict majority of slots agrees on a result (either positive or negative), the smart contract flags the data entry as \texttt{VALID} or \texttt{INVALID}, and the verification process concludes.
Any remaining unfilled slots are no longer needed and are effectively discarded.
If \texttt{V} cannot complete the task or misses the deadline, it can submit the special result \texttt{CANNOT\_COMPLETE}; in this case, the verification slot is released and becomes available for reassignment.
Once the data entry is flagged, payments and penalties are applied, as described in Section~\ref{sec:incentive-mechanisms}.

\section{Incentive mechanisms}\label{sec:incentive-mechanisms}
The incentive mechanism consists of two different approaches: rewarding actors to encourage honest behaviour and penalizing actors discouraging dishonest behaviours.
These approaches are enforced through a rewarding system (Section~\ref{subsec:proto-rewarding-model}) and a bond system (Section~\ref{subsec:proto-bond-model}).

\subsection{Data Sent Token (DST)}\label{subsec:data-sent-token}
Data Sent Token (DST) is a custom ERC-20~\cite{eip20} token used to incentivize participation in the campaign, following the approach of reward-based crowdsensing incentive schemes such as~\cite{ying2025chaser}.

\subsection{Rewarding system}\label{subsec:proto-rewarding-model}
The rewarding system is  configurable by \texttt{B}.
The working process is quite straightforward: when a data entry is flagged as \texttt{VALID} or \texttt{INVALID} the rewarding process is triggered:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item If the data is \texttt{VALID}, the smart contract rewards \texttt{S} with the conFigured payout. If the data is \texttt{INVALID}, \texttt{S} receives no payout.
    \item Verifiers, on the other hand, are rewarded conditionally: only verifiers who voted according to the final verification result receive the conFigured payout. Verifiers who submitted a contradicting vote receive no reward.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Bond system}\label{subsec:proto-bond-model}
A bond is a refundable collateral, denominated in DST tokens, that senders and verifiers must deposit before contributing to the campaign.
It serves as a commitment device: honest behaviour results in the bond being returned, while invalid contributions or incorrect verifications lead to its forfeit.

The bond system, like the rewarding one, is fully configurable by \texttt{B}, and operates alongside the rewarding system (Section~\ref{subsec:proto-rewarding-model}).
Bond tokens are refunded together with the associated reward, in order to minimize the number of transactions and the overall gas cost.
Conversely, when a data entry is flagged as \texttt{INVALID}, the sender's bond is forfeited; similarly, verifiers whose vote contradicts the final verification result forfeit their bond.

The bond mechanism differs between senders and verifiers.
Verifiers deposit a bond equal to \texttt{maxDataPerVerifierPerBond} $\times$ \texttt{dataVerifierBondPerData}, which covers multiple verifications; when the bond is insufficient, the transaction is reverted and the verifier can top it up and retry.
Senders, on the other hand, are required to deposit \texttt{maxDataPerSender} $\times$ \texttt{dataSenderBondPerData} upfront.
Since the bond decreases by \texttt{dataSenderBondPerData} for every submission and cannot be topped up, it effectively caps the number of contributions a sender can make per campaign.

For this reason, the bond payment constitutes an intermediate phase between enrollment and data submission.
Bond payment is located in the complete workflow as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:proto-design-pay-bond}.
All parameters shown in this section are explained in Table~\ref{tab:params-needs}

\section{Security analysis and other issues}\label{sec:security-analysis-issues}
In this section the security properties of the protocol are analyzed and known limitations are discussed.
The following sections discuss how to address the exposed issues and limitations of this protocol, or their extension.

\subsection{Formal verification (ProVerif)}\label{subsec:security-analysis-issues-proverif}
The presented protocol has been formally verified using ProVerif and is secure under the Dolev-Yao threat model~\cite{dolev1983security, blanchet2001proverif} if all the actors behave honestly:
an external adversary $\mathcal{A}$ cannot recover the plaintext $CID$ from $CID_{ciphered}$ and cannot decrypt the data stored on IPFS.
Modeling and execution are described in the Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-b-proverif-v1-honest}.

\subsection{Malicious verifier attack}\label{subsec:security-analysis-issues-verifier-attack}
In the current protocol, the adversary $\mathcal{A}$ can join as a verifier and if the campaign initiator $B$ accepts them as a verifier, then $\mathcal{A}$ is able to read all the data transmitted, regardless of which verifier is elected for a specific data entry.
This follows from the public visibility of $CID_{ciphered}$ values on the blockchain and the shared $K_{dec}$ among all enrolled verifiers.
Since all transactions are publicly visible on the blockchain, $\mathcal{A}$ can observe every $CID_{ciphered}$ submitted by any sender and can decrypt $CID_{ciphered}$ and the corresponding data using $K_{dec}$.
This issue requires that the senders must trust not only the campaign initiator, but also every enrolled verifier.
The attack is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:proto-design-malicious-verifier-attack}.

\subsection{Geographic incentive imbalance}\label{subsec:security-analysis-issues-unused-geography}
The proposed protocol introduces a coordinate system and uses it to partition collected data into geographic chunks.
Consider the following scenario: \texttt{B} starts a campaign in an area where some chunks cover urban zones and others cover rural ones.
Since urban areas have significantly more inhabitants than rural ones, it is reasonable to assume that data submissions concentrate in chunks covering cities.
In the current protocol, the rewarding system offers no additional incentive to collect data in less populated areas.
Furthermore, the privacy of senders in rural areas is more at risk due to lower population density, as fewer submissions per chunk make individual contributions easier to identify,
a concern consistent with the broader spatio-temporal privacy risks in MCS~\cite{montori2023privacy}.
The current protocol does not differentiate incentives by geographic density, leaving rural coverage and the associated privacy risk unaddressed.
A possible extension could introduce density-aware reward multipliers, encouraging submissions in underrepresented chunks while discouraging over-contribution in saturated ones.

\subsection{Centrality of B}\label{subsec:security-analysis-issues-centrality-b}
The centrality of the campaign initiator can be considered a threat to the protocol, but it is also essential to its design: \texttt{B} funds the campaign by paying for data collection and the verification process.
\texttt{B} has a direct economic interest in behaving honestly; moreover, the \texttt{totalDst} amount (described in Table~\ref{tab:params-rewards}) is locked in the smart contract and cannot be withdrawn by \texttt{B}.
However, if \texttt{B}'s private key $SK_B$ is compromised, an adversary gains the ability to decrypt all actors' public keys and forge key deliveries, compromising the entire campaign.
Distributing \texttt{B}'s role through techniques such as threshold cryptography~\cite{shamir1979share} or multi-party key generation could mitigate this risk and is left as a possible future extension.

\subsection{Address linkability}\label{subsec:security-analysis-issues-address-linkability}
Senders interact with the smart contract through a single account and cannot change their address throughout the campaign.
As a consequence, all submissions from the same sender are linkable: an observer can count the number of data entries a specific address has submitted and infer activity patterns.
If a sender reaches the \texttt{maxDataPerSender} limit (described in Table~\ref{tab:params-needs}) and wishes to continue contributing, they must create a new account and deposit the bond again.
The transfer of DST should be made with privacy-first currencies in DEX swappers for unlink the new account to the old one~\cite{montori2023privacy}.
If the sender behaved honestly during the first submissions, the bond is fully refunded and can be reused for the new account.
This trade-off was accepted deliberately: using a single address simplifies bond accounting and enables the \texttt{maxDataPerSender} cap, at the cost of submission linkability.

\subsection{Verifier penalties}\label{subsec:security-analysis-verifiers-no-penalties}
In the current protocol, the only penalty a verifier faces for incorrect behaviour is the loss of the deposited bond, which may be insufficient to deter sustained malicious activity.
A possible improvement would introduce a reputation mechanism: past incorrect verifications should be recorded and forgiven only after $n$ subsequent correct verifications, or the verifier should be removed after $m$ accumulated incorrect verifications.
This ensures, on the one hand, that the verifier maintains honest behaviour over time, and, on the other, that occasional mistakes do not lead to immediate removal.

Furthermore, a verification slot manipulation attack is possible.
Since all verification results are publicly visible on the blockchain, an elected verifier can delay its
submission and observe the votes of other verifiers.
Once the majority outcome becomes apparent, the verifier submits a vote
aligned with the majority just before the timeout, guaranteeing
the reward without performing any actual data validation.

A robust solution would require a commit-reveal scheme~\cite{brassard1983commitreveal, eip1011}, where verifiers first commit a hash of their vote and later reveal the actual result.
This would prevent verifiers from observing others' votes before submitting their own.
However, implementing commit-reveal on-chain introduces additional gas costs and protocol complexity, and is left as a future extension.

\subsection{Sybil attack}\label{subsec:security-analysis-verifier-sybil}
In the context of decentralized protocols, a Sybil attack occurs when a single adversary creates multiple identities to gain disproportionate influence over the system~\cite{douceur2002sybil}.
In this protocol, a Sybil attack targets the verifier role: an adversary could create enough identities to control multiple verification slots,with two possible goals: accessing all campaign data without legitimate
participation, or accumulating a majority of slots to drain rewards by forcing arbitrary verification outcomes.

The Sybil attack is partially mitigated by two mechanisms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Economic barrier}: each verifier identity must deposit
    a bond of \newline $n \times \texttt{dataVerifierBondPerData} \times
    \texttt{maxDataPerVerifierPerBond}$ DST, raising the cost of
    the attack proportionally to the number of identities created~\cite{chatzopoulos2018privacy, ying2025chaser};
    \item \textbf{Enrollment control}: all verifiers must be accepted
    by \texttt{B} during enrollment, providing an off-chain vetting
    opportunity.
\end{itemize}

However, these defenses may be insufficient against a well-funded adversary (e.g., a state actor) for whom the bond cost is negligible.
In such a scenario, the primary defense remains \texttt{B}'s ability to vet verifier enrollment requests through off-chain identity verification mechanisms.

\section{Addressing issues}\label{sec:addressing-issues}
In this section some solution at design-level are proposed to solve the issues described in
Section~\ref{subsec:security-analysis-issues-verifier-attack},
Section~\ref{subsec:security-analysis-issues-unused-geography}
and Section~\ref{subsec:security-analysis-verifiers-no-penalties}.

\subsection{Addressing trusted verifier issue}\label{subsec:addressing-issues-trusted-verifier-issue}
From the sender's perspective, the proposed protocol requires trusting not  only \texttt{B} but all current and future verifiers.
This represents a strong discouragement for senders, as their data can be accessed by any enrolled verifier through the public blockchain, particularly if \texttt{B} accepts a malicious verifier.
This issue can be addressed by modifying the verifier enrollment logic, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:proto-data-enrollment-verification-novel}, and the data submission phase, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:proto-data-submission-verification-workflow}.

The idea is straightforward: during verifier enrollment, \texttt{B} retains the  public key of every verifier locally.
When a datum is submitted, \texttt{B} is notified through an event and generates a unique asymmetric key pair $(KD_{enc}, KD_{dec})$ for that data entry.
Subsequently, \texttt{B} decrypts $CID_{ciphered}$ using $K_{dec}$ to obtain the original $CID$, retrieves the  encrypted data from IPFS, and decrypts it using $K_{dec}$.
\texttt{B} then  re-encrypts the data using $KD_{enc}$ and uploads it to IPFS, obtaining a new  content identifier $CID_V$.
\texttt{B} concludes this step by notifying the smart contract that the per-datum key material is ready.

Once the smart contract is informed by \texttt{B}, it emits an event that verifiers monitor; upon receiving it, they can request verification authorization.
When a verifier is elected to a slot, the smart contract notifies \texttt{B}, who constructs a \texttt{verification\_bundle} containing $CID_V$ and $KD_{dec}$, encrypted using the elected verifier's public key $PK_V$.
Finally, \texttt{B} delivers the bundle to the smart contract, which emits an event notifying the elected verifier.

This extension eliminates the shared $K_{dec}$ vulnerability: each verifier can only access data entries for which it has been specifically elected.
However, this comes at the cost of increased workload for \texttt{B}, who must perform one re-encryption cycle per data entry, and additional IPFS storage, as each datum now exists in two encrypted forms.
