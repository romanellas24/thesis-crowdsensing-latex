\chapter{Evaluation}\label{ch:evaluation}

The protocols designed in Chapter~\ref{ch:protocol-design} and implemented in
Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation} are evaluated along two complementary axes.
Section~\ref{sec:gas-analysis} analyses the gas consumption of the three
contract variants, across a $4 \times 4$ scenario grid of sender and verifier counts, quantifying the cost
of the swap-and-pop optimisation and the overhead introduced by the novel
protocol extensions.
Section~\ref{sec:proto-evaluation-concurrent-execution} examines the behaviour
of the network under concurrent campaign load, showing that the per-block gas
limit introduces a critical section that forces partial serialisation when
multiple campaigns execute simultaneously.
All analyses are expressed in absolute units of measure, such as gas units for
computational cost and DST tokens for economic rewards.
Finally, Section~\ref{sec:spatial-simulation} evaluates the behaviour of the protocol on a dataset of taxi traces from the city of Porto.


\section{Gas cost analysis}\label{sec:gas-analysis}
There are three contracts under analysis: \texttt{PFC}, \texttt{PSC}, and \texttt{FIFO}.
\texttt{FIFO} shares the same base implementation as \texttt{PFC} but replaces the swap-and-pop pending list with a linear-shift FIFO approach.

The methodology of these experiments is described in Section~\ref{subsec:gas-analysis-methodology}.
Section~\ref{subsec:gas-pfc-vs-fifo} presents a comparison between the swap-and-pop data structure and FIFO queue.
Section~\ref{subsec:gas-pfc-vs-psc} contains a comparison to show the overhead introduced by the novel features, i.e., the cost of extending \texttt{PFC} to \texttt{PSC}.

Finally, Section~\ref{subsec:gas-real-world-costs} estimates the cost of organizing a crowdsensing campaign in USD.

\subsection{Methodology}\label{subsec:gas-analysis-methodology}
The test environment is based on Hardhat, the experiments are executed on a simulated Ethereum environment on the \texttt{cancun} hardfork was selected to avoid the per-transaction gas cap of $2^{24}$ gas introduced by EIP-7825~\cite{eip7825} in the Fusaka hard fork.
In addition, the gas limit per block is modified to $10^9$ to avoid interruption on worst-case.

A grid of scenarios, a grid made with a dimension of $4\times 4$, the scenarios are a combination between
\begin{gather*}
    \forall S \in \{1, 10, 100, 1000\}\\
    \forall V \in \{2, 10, 100, 1000\}\\
\end{gather*}

\subsection{PFC and FIFO pending list management}\label{subsec:gas-pfc-vs-fifo}

\begin{figure}

    \caption{FIFO baseline infeasibility under EIP-7825 across all scenarios.}
    \label{fig:proto-evaluation-eip7825-feasibility}
\end{figure}

\texttt{PrivacyFirstContract} applies a swap-and-pop approach to the \texttt{\_removeFromPending} function.
Deletion runs in $O(1)$, but order is not preserved.

In this context, it is necessary to introduce a new smart contract implementing a FIFO queue for pending data awaiting verification, as shown in Listing~\ref{lst:chap-c-fifo-contract}.
This smart contract is a variant that inherits all functions from \texttt{PFC} and overrides \texttt{\_addToPending} and \texttt{\_removeFromPending} to implement a FIFO strategy.

For the \texttt{verification\_submit} function, Figure~\ref{fig:proto-evaluation-pfc-vs-fifo-submit-overlay} plots the gas consumption per call for the \texttt{PFC} and \texttt{FIFO} contracts.
The comparison is clear: \texttt{PFC} gas usage falls in the range $[120, 300]\,\text{k}$ gas units and becomes constant after 2\,500 submissions.

For the \texttt{FIFO} contract the situation is different: there are two types of submissions: those that do not complete a verification process, and those that trigger one.
If a verification submission completes a verification process, the verified data is removed from the pending data structure; \texttt{\_removeFromPending} is therefore called, and in the \texttt{FIFO} contract this causes a left-shift of every subsequent entry.

Additionally, the two contracts differ in gas usage for the following functions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{verification\_request}: the \texttt{FIFO} contract must iterate more times to satisfy the cooldown constraint;
    \item \texttt{data\_submission}: \texttt{FIFO} is cheaper than \texttt{PFC} by $22\,\text{k}$ gas units.
\end{itemize}

Despite the \texttt{data\_submission} savings, PFC's advantage of $14\,\text{M}$ gas units per \texttt{verification\_submit} call far outweighs the $22\,\text{k} \times 10000 = 220\,\text{M}$ gas units saved by FIFO across all data submissions.

Figure~\ref{fig:proto-evaluation-eip7825-feasibility} shows the ratio of worst-case \texttt{verification\_submit} gas to the
EIP-7825 cap, for each of the 16 scenarios.

Values above $1.0$ indicate scenarios where the contract is infeasible on a Fusaka-era chain.
\texttt{PrivacyFirstContract} stays consistently below $0.02$, two orders of magnitude under the cap.
\texttt{PrivacyFirstContractFifo} exceeds the cap in all scenarios by a factor of $\approx 3.4\times$.

The ratio is independent of \((senders, verifiers)\) because the
worst-case shift cost is driven by \texttt{TOTAL\_CIDS}, not by the
actor count.

A full comparison of gas usage between the two protocols is shown in Table~\ref{tab:pfc-vs-fifo-max-submit}. For all scenarios, the maximum gas for both protocols is participant-independent, as evidenced by identical values across all rows.
For the \texttt{FIFO} contract the ratio with the EIP-7825 limit exceeds one, so with $10,000$ CIDs \texttt{FIFO} is not feasible.

\subsection{PFC and PSC feature overhead}\label{subsec:gas-pfc-vs-psc}
\begin{figure}
    \centering

    \caption{Operations common to PFC and PSC. Percentage deltas
    annotated on each pair.}

\end{figure}

\texttt{PrivacySecondContract} inherits \texttt{PrivacyFirstContract} functions and implements the features described in Section~\ref{sec:addressing-issues}.

Each feature has a measurable gas cost; this section therefore evaluates the overhead of each implemented feature and identifies which contributes most.

Figure~\ref{fig:proto-evaluation-psc-overhead-common} shows the variation in gas usage for functions common to both contracts.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Deployment}: bytecode size increases by $15.8\%$ due to the additional bytecode;
    \item \texttt{data\_submission}: gas usage increases by $20.7\%$ due to the computation of the decayed reward for each submission;
    \item \texttt{verification\_submit}: increases by $9.1\%$ due to verifier vote tracking (correct and incorrect) and a newly introduced \texttt{require} check;
    \item \texttt{owner\_send\_kdec}: gas usage decreases by $55.5\%$, as the function now only emits an event, registers a mapping for the accepted verifier, and stores the Verifier struct.
\end{itemize}

\texttt{PrivacySecondContract} does not alter the behaviour of existing functions, but introduces new ones with their own costs, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:proto-evaluation-psc-overhead-only}.
The three new functions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{owner\_notify\_key\_ready}: consumes $76\,\text{k}$ gas units after the creation of the per-data key pair.
    \item \texttt{owner\_deliver\_bundle}: uses $63\,\text{k}$ gas units to deliver the verification bundle: containing the per-data decryption key to the verifier.
    \item \texttt{owner\_kick\_verifier}: penalty path, invoked by the owner against malicious verifiers; costs $30\,\text{k}$ gas units.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Real-world deployment costs across networks}\label{subsec:gas-real-world-costs}
Gas costs exist to prevent replay attacks and to incentivize verifiers to stake their funds and validate new blocks~\cite{wood2014ethereum}.
The formula to evaluate the USD cost of a certain gas usage is the following:
\begin{equation}
    \text{GAS}_{\text{COST}} = \text{GAS}_{\text{USED}} \times \text{GAS}_{\text{PRICE}} \times \text{TOKEN}_{\text{COST}}
    \label{eq:proto-evaluation-gas-costs}
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:proto-evaluation-gas-costs} explains how the $\text{GAS}_{\text{COST}}$ is calculated as a function of the gas used, the gas price, and the native network token cost.

Tables~\ref{tab:proto-evaluation-merged-fifo},~\ref{tab:proto-evaluation-merged-pfc} and ~\ref{tab:proto-evaluation-merged-psc} show a comparison between the roles in the $1000\,\text{S} \times 1000\,\text{V}$ scenario, respectively for \texttt{FIFO}, \texttt{PFC} and \texttt{PSC} contracts.
For verifiers and senders, the gas is calculated per actor; the mean, median, minimum, and maximum gas usage are reported.
In addition, for each network all gas usage data are reported, while the corresponding gas cost reflects the selected network

\section{Block Saturation Under Concurrent Campaigns}\label{sec:proto-evaluation-concurrent-execution}
The Ethereum blockchain enforces a per-block gas limit, set to $60\text{,}000\text{,}000$
gas units under the Fusaka hard fork~\cite{eip7935}, which bounds the total computational
work that validators may include in a single block~\cite{wood2014ethereum}.
This experiment aims to verify whether this constraint introduces a critical section on
block capacity when multiple campaigns execute concurrently.
To this end, the block count consumed by each campaign is measured as a function of the number of concurrent campaigns~$N$.

\subsection{Gas Consumption Baseline}\label{subsec:proto-evaluation-concurrent-execution-baseline}
The gas consumption of every contract is reported in
Tables~\ref{tab:proto-evaluation-merged-fifo},~\ref{tab:proto-evaluation-merged-pfc},~and~\ref{tab:proto-evaluation-merged-psc}.
\texttt{PFC} is the contract with the lowest gas consumption.
This experiment was conducted using \texttt{PFC}, because if multiple concurrent
executions of \texttt{PFC} exhibit a critical section over the per-block gas limit,
then contracts with a higher gas consumption will necessarily exhibit the same contention.
For this reason, \texttt{PFC} was chosen as the baseline.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}\label{subsec:proto-evaluation-concurrent-execution-setup}
The simulation was executed on a local Hardhat EVM node configured with a block
gas limit of $60\text{,}000\text{,}000$ gas~\cite{eip7935} and interval mining
at $200\,\text{ms}$, so that blocks are produced at a fixed cadence regardless
of mempool pressure.
Between each batch of $N$ campaigns, the chain state was
reset via \texttt{hardhat\_reset} to ensure independent measurements.

Each campaign runs the \texttt{PFC} contract with $1\text{,}000$ senders,
$100$ verifiers, $5\text{,}000$ CIDs, and \texttt{checksRequired}$= 3$.
Every actor belongs to exactly one campaign; no wallets are shared across
campaigns.
Concurrency is achieved by launching all $N$ campaign runners
inside a single \texttt{Promise.all}, so that transactions from different
campaigns interleave in the mempool and compete for block space under the
shared gas limit.
The values of $N$ evaluated are $N \in \{1, 2, 4\}$.

The metric recorded for each campaign is the number of Ethereum blocks
consumed, defined as $\Delta b = b_{\mathrm{end}} - b_{\mathrm{start}}$,
where $b_{\mathrm{start}}$ is the block number snapshotted immediately before
\texttt{Promise.all} fires and $b_{\mathrm{end}}$ is the block number of the
last confirmed transaction of that campaign. Wall-clock time is intentionally
excluded, as it reflects the speed of the local Hardhat node rather than
mainnet behaviour.

\subsection{Results and analysis}\label{subsec:proto-evaluation-concurrent-execution-results-analysis}

Table~\ref{tab:concurrent-blocks} reports the mean block count consumed per
campaign as a function of $N$ for \texttt{PFC}, together with the individual
per-campaign values.

transactions.

\section{Spatial simulation}\label{sec:spatial-simulation}
Section~\ref{subsec:addressing-issues-geographical-incentive-issue} describes a
mechanism to address the geographical incentive imbalance.
The following simulation aims to empirically validate the implemented solution,
using the Taxi Trajectory dataset from Porto~\cite{taxi_trajectory_ecml2015}.

\subsection{Experimental setup}\label{subsec:spatial-setup}
The experiment is conducted over the city of Porto using the taxi trajectory
dataset~\cite{taxi_trajectory_ecml2015}.
The grid comprises $10$ chunks per side, for a total of $100$ chunks.
The campaign area is a square of approximately $28\,\text{km}$ per side, where
each chunk covers approximately $2{.}8\,\text{km} \times 2{.}1\,\text{km}$.
The origin of the area, located at the south-west corner, is at
$41.14\text{°N}$, $8.61\text{°W}$.
The experiment is conducted with $500$ and $1\text{,}000$ senders; since
coverage increases by only $3$ chunks between the two configurations, only the
results for the $1\text{,}000$-sender scenario are presented.
Each taxi trajectory is associated with a distinct sender, and each chunk
accepts a maximum of $10\text{,}000$ data entries.

underrepresented chunks in order to maximise their reward.

Section~\ref{subsec:extended-protocol-introducing-geographical-incentives} is
correct.

\section{Result and discussion}\label{sec:result-discussion}

The experiments presented in this chapter lead to several conclusions.

Section~\ref{sec:gas-analysis} compares the three contract variants.
\texttt{FIFO} is infeasible under EIP-7825~\cite{eip7825}: its O($n$) pending
list removal causes the worst-case gas of \texttt{submitVerifyResult} to exceed
the per-transaction cap already at moderate CID counts.
\texttt{PFC} is the most efficient contract, but it does not address the issues
analysed in Section~\ref{sec:security-analysis-issues}.
\texttt{PSC} addresses those issues at a measurable but controlled overhead: the
additional gas introduced by its three extensions remains within the same order
of magnitude as \texttt{PFC}.
Across all three contracts, deployment and operation on Ethereum mainnet is
expensive for the campaign initiator, while sender and verifier costs are
acceptable.
On L2 networks the costs become negligible for all roles.

Section~\ref{sec:proto-evaluation-concurrent-execution} shows that $N$ concurrent
campaigns produce a critical section on block capacity.
In production, concurrent campaigns should be staggered or deployed on L2 networks
with a higher block gas limit or a shorter block time to reduce contention.

Section~\ref{sec:spatial-simulation} confirms that the reward decay mechanism
operates as designed.
The 50\% spatial coverage observed on the Porto dataset reflects the intrinsic
geographic concentration of the taxi fleet rather than a protocol limitation.
The decay parameter $k$ controls the steepness of the incentive curve: higher
values produce a gentler decay, preserving a meaningful reward even for
highly saturated chunks, while lower values more aggressively redirect senders
towards underrepresented areas.
